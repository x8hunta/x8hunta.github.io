The Complacent Operator: Designing the Human-Digital Handshake

Automation detects. Accountable people resolve, together and with evidence.

What happened

This is a simulated scenario I designed for practice. It follows on from the power grid dependency.

The independent heat sensors from the previous challenge do their job. They disagree with the control system and put the grid into its safe state. A junior engineer decides the sensor is “glitchy” and switches the system back to automatic mode, even though the risk is still high.

The technician’s view

The engineer made a mistake or didn’t understand the risk. Give staff more training, or add a warning pop-up before an override.

People click through warning pop-ups. Training fades. Neither changes the fact that one person, going on a hunch, could switch off a safety system.

The architect’s view

This is a permission failure. The process gave a single person too much discretion, and it gave him nothing objective to judge the sensor with. “Glitchy” was an opinion, not a measurement.

The control: Quorum Override with an Accountability Record

  1. Evidence-backed quorum: an override needs three people to sign: the operator, a technical authority and a risk officer. They must attach physical evidence, such as photos of the gauges.
  2. Sensor confidence scores: each sensor carries a score based on its calibration history, signal noise and track record. When a high-confidence sensor disagrees with the control system, overriding it takes more evidence, not less.
  3. Permanent record: every override is logged in a record that can’t be edited. The signers explicitly acknowledge that they’re choosing the software’s reading over the physical one.

How to prove it works

Pull the override log. Every entry should show three distinct signers, attached evidence and the sensor’s confidence score at the time. Then try overriding with one signature in a test environment and confirm it’s refused.

Takeaway

Don’t design a safety system that depends on everyone having a good day. Design it so no single bad call can switch it off.
