The Profit-Driven Bypass: When the Rules Protect the Rule-Breakers

Separate the people who benefit from a risk from the people who approve it.

What happened

This is a simulated scenario I designed for practice. The bank and the figures are invented.

At a bank, a highly profitable high-frequency trading team set up its own server outside the official asset register. Security flagged it. Instead of shutting it down, the Chief Information Officer relabeled it a “legacy exception” so the team could keep making money. The unmanaged server eventually led to a $400 million loss.

The technician’s view

The server wasn’t patched and the firewall was bypassed. Patch it and tighten the firewall rules.

The server wasn’t the real problem. Leadership saw it, understood it and chose to keep it.

The architect’s view

The failure happened when the asset first appeared, at the identification stage. The bank knew the server existed but never brought it under governance. Profit was treated as a valid substitute for security.

The pillar that failed was metadata. The server’s record was changed to hide its risk. A phrase that sounds professional, “legacy exception”, turned a red flag into a grey one. I call this a metadata blind spot: using the language of the record to cover a liability.

The control: Independent Oversight Mandate

  1. Independent sign-off: every security exception must be approved by a committee (the chief risk officer, the chief information security officer and the external auditor). None of them can report to the person who benefits from the exception.
  2. Hard expiry dates: every exception is temporary. It’s automatically flagged for shutdown unless it’s justified again.
  3. Personal accountability: knowingly approving an unregistered asset is treated as a breach of the approver’s duty to the organization.

How to prove it works

List every open exception. For each one, check who approved it, whether any approver reports to the person who benefits, and whether it’s past its expiry date. Then compare the network against the asset register. Any device on the network that isn’t in the register is a finding.

Takeaway

When the rules are bent to protect the people breaking them, the problem isn’t a server. It’s the institution. No one should be too profitable to govern.
